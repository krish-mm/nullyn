Nullyn AI: low-latency feedback suppression

In preparation — full paper and data release after review

The draft paper, Toward Low-Latency Neural Feedback Suppression for Live Music, reviews the published evidence and sets out how Nullyn AI will be measured. It has not been peer reviewed, and Nullyn AI’s own results have not been reported yet.

Abstract

Acoustic feedback — the howl when a microphone picks up its own loudspeaker — limits how loud a live sound system can run. Fifty years of research has produced three main families of automatic control: frequency shifting and phase modulation, notch-filter howling suppression, and adaptive feedback cancellation. In the field, many sound engineers still prefer to control feedback by hand because automatic systems are not reliable enough. Since 2022, neural networks have been applied to howling suppression; the studies reviewed for this paper evaluate on speech, mostly with simulated loop delays of 100 to 500 ms, and most report speech-separation scores rather than the stable-gain measures the feedback field uses.

Live music is different. Instruments and sung notes are tonal, and that is exactly what classical howl detectors confuse with feedback. Performers on in-ear monitors can notice added delay of a few milliseconds. This paper sets out that evidence, names the gaps a live-music system has to close, and describes Nullyn AI: two inputs — the microphone and a reference of the speaker feed from the mixer — a per-sample digital filter path at 96 kHz, which adds about one sample (10.4 µs) of processing delay, and a neural control path that runs alongside the audio. The architecture is closed-loop: Nullyn AI takes a feed of what goes to the speakers and uses it to cancel feedback, with an AI notch as fallback, so the loop can keep working if someone changes the mixer. Nullyn AI’s analog-in to analog-out delay is set by the converters. Datasheet arithmetic on a PCM3060-class codec gives about 0.4 ms at 96 kHz, not 10 µs. Nullyn AI’s own results will be added when they are produced.

Methodology

Nullyn AI sits in-line with two inputs — the microphone and a speaker-feed reference from the mixer — a per-sample DSP filter path at 96 kHz, and XLR/TRS out. It uses the speaker feed to cancel feedback, with an AI notch as fallback, and is intended to keep working if the mixer changes. A neural control path running alongside the audio decides how the filter path should be set. The audio never passes through the network; the network writes filter coefficients. Nullyn AI is designed around off-the-shelf parts and an open toolchain, with a PCM3060-class codec as the converter reference.

Nullyn AI is evaluated first in a closed-loop simulator with the same signal chain as the hardware. The protocol uses established metrics where they exist: maximum and added stable gain, path latency, detection, howl and recovery time, and distortion on speech and on music. Tests include a fader push for gain-before-feedback, a gain step for detection and recovery, a feedback-path change when a microphone moves, clean-program distortion, and an impulse-response latency measurement.

A larger scenario sweep applies that same closed-loop model across weddings, conferences, and live shows, including cases where people and microphones move, and compares each run with Nullyn AI in the path and with it out. The sweep is in progress. Results will be labelled as simulated or measured when they are published with the paper.
